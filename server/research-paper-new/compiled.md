Introduction
Modern urban centers worldwide grapple with increasingly complex transportation challenges, marked by pervasive traffic congestion that significantly impedes economic productivity, degrades environmental quality, and diminishes the overall quality of life for residents. This predicament is particularly acute in rapidly developing urban regions, such as Kathmandu and Lalitpur, Nepal, which face the dual pressures of burgeoning populations and escalating vehicle ownership. Despite continuous efforts in infrastructure development and traffic management, these cities experience persistent delays, heightened fuel consumption, and increased risks of accidents on their critical road networks. The core issue lies in the inherent unpredictability of urban environments, where sudden incidents—ranging from traffic accidents and unexpected road closures to temporary hazards—render traditional navigation systems, often reliant on static or historical traffic data, inherently inadequate. A pressing need thus exists for navigation solutions capable of offering real-time responsiveness and verifiable safety in such dynamic, obstacle-rich urban landscapes.
The central problem addressed by this research is the critical demand for a pathfinding algorithm capable of generating **real-time, verifiably safe, and efficient routes** within dense urban networks characterized by the presence of dynamic obstacles. While high-performance routing algorithms like Contraction Hierarchies excel in static environments by leveraging extensive pre-computation, their rigid nature renders them unsuitable for scenarios where road conditions fluctuate unpredictably due to dynamic impediments. Conversely, more adaptable heuristic search methods, such as A\*, often lack mechanisms to guarantee strict adherence to safety margins, or they incur prohibitive computational costs when attempting to incorporate such stringent criteria. This creates a significant research gap: an absence of a solution that effectively harmonizes computational speed for immediate responsiveness, path efficiency to minimize detours, search reliability to ensure path discovery, and, most critically, **provable safety** to strictly avoid obstacles within defined boundaries.
To bridge this gap, this paper introduces an \*enhanced Bidirectional A\ algorithm** specifically engineered for real-time urban navigation. Its foundational innovation is a **novel hard exclusion mechanism\*\*, which fundamentally differs from traditional soft-penalty approaches. This mechanism rigorously prunes any path that violates a user-defined safety margin around dynamic obstacles directly from the search space, thereby providing verifiable safety guarantees essential for critical applications. Integrated with efficient spatial indexing, which ensures rapid proximity queries against dynamic obstacle locations, this algorithm is designed to concurrently optimize real-time performance, path efficiency, search reliability, and provable safety. Our contributions encompass the development and empirical validation of this hard exclusion strategy, a comprehensive quantitative analysis of its multi-objective trade-offs across varying obstacle densities, and an empirical demonstration of its real-time feasibility on a complex urban road network. This work presents a robust framework poised to advance the capabilities of next-generation navigation systems.
Problem Statement
Pathfinding in dynamic urban environments is a complex challenge that sits at the intersection of classical graph search theory, real-time systems, and dynamic obstacle avoidance. This review surveys recent literature to contextualize the contributions of this work, focusing on modern enhancements to A\*-based algorithms and strategies for handling dynamic obstacles in real-world navigation.
2.1. Enhancements to A\* and Heuristic Search
The A\* algorithm remains a foundational component of modern pathfinding, but its classic implementation faces significant performance challenges in large-scale or complex environments. A systematic literature review by Foead et al. [Foead et al., 2021] confirms that classic A\* is often inadequate for modern demands, suffering from performance degradation and high memory overhead on large maps. This has spurred extensive research into A\* variants. For instance, Multi-Heuristic A\* (MHA\*) addresses the issue of "heuristic depression regions" by running multiple, simultaneous searches guided by different heuristics (both admissible and inadmissible), which has been shown to achieve significant speedups over Weighted A\* in complex robotics domains [Aine et al., 2015]. Further work has focused on improving search efficiency by modifying the evaluation function itself. One approach involves using dynamic weights that adapt as the search progresses; for example, giving more weight to the heuristic early in the search and more weight to the actual cost as the goal is approached, which has been shown to reduce traversed nodes and search time significantly [Li et al., 2023]. Other methods improve efficiency by eliminating redundant computations. Instead of backtracking when an obstacle is hit, an optimized A\* can simply mark the node as impassable (cost = ∞) and select the next-best child node, a strategy that has demonstrated up to a 70% reduction in path-planning time in complex environments [Liu, Wang, & Xu, 2022].
2.2. Strategies for Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance
Handling dynamic obstacles is a primary challenge in real-time navigation. A common theme in the literature is the use of cost penalties or potential fields to represent obstacles. The Artificial Potential Field (APF) method, where the agent is guided by attractive forces from the goal and repulsive forces from obstacles, is a classic approach. However, it is prone to local minima. Recent research has focused on hybrid methods that combine a global planner like A\* with a local, reactive method like APF or the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA). In such a hybrid architecture, A\* generates a global path, which is then used to guide a local planner that can react to immediate, unforeseen obstacles [Zhang et al., 2025; Liu, Wang, & Xu, 2022]. This decouples the strategic global plan from the tactical, real-time avoidance maneuver.
The concept of treating obstacles as sources of "cost" or "risk" is pervasive. In vehicular ad-hoc networks (VANETs), traffic density is often used as a direct proxy for obstacle-aware penalties, with routing decisions based on a dynamic score combining density and distance [Jerbi et al., 2006, as cited in your analysis]. A similar approach models traffic light delays by translating waiting times into an equivalent distance cost, which is then added to the A\* heuristic function, successfully guiding the search toward routes with fewer interruptions [Hu, Yang, & Huang, 2016]. More explicit penalty functions have been used in real-time navigation systems, where a large penalty factor is added to the utility function for any road segment reported as blocked by crowd-sourced data, effectively making the path prohibitively expensive [Wan, Ghazzai, & Massoud, 2019]. These "soft penalty" methods stand in contrast to our work's focus on a "hard exclusion" strategy.
2.3. Real-Time Routing in Dynamic Urban Networks
The specific challenges of urban networks, dominated by traffic congestion, have been extensively studied. A review by Isa et al. [Isa, Mohamed, & Yusoff, 2015] emphasizes the necessity of online, dynamic routing that can adapt to both recurrent (peak hour) and non-recurrent (accident) congestion. Many modern approaches treat traffic congestion itself as a dynamic obstacle. Self-adaptive evolutionary algorithms have been developed for Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problems (DVRP) that explicitly model congestion by adding a dynamic "traffic factor" to travel times between locations, allowing the algorithm to evolve solutions that are robust to changing traffic conditions [Sabar et al., 2018]. Another approach uses a multi-agent framework where individual vehicles play a "congestion game," learning from experience to select routes that maximize their personal utility, which is a function of driver preference, cost, and an "uncertainty value" for unexpected incidents [Yan, Hu, & Hu, 2018]. These works underscore the trend toward adaptive, data-driven routing that responds to real-time conditions. The use of real-time crowd-sourced data, as proposed by Wan et al. [Wan, Ghazzai, & Massoud, 2019], provides a direct data pipeline for such systems, enabling them to react instantly to user-reported blockages by re-calculating routes within a specified time window.

2.4. Synthesis and Research Gap
The literature confirms a clear and consistent trend toward more intelligent, adaptive, and context-aware pathfinding algorithms. It is well-established that classic A\* requires modification to be efficient in large, dynamic environments [Foead et al., 2021; Jern & Salomonsson, 2024], and a variety of methods exist for incorporating dynamic information, primarily through "soft" cost penalties within a heuristic or cost function [Hu, Yang, & Huang, 2016; Sabar et al., 2018; Wan, Ghazzai, & Massoud, 2019]. Hybrid architectures that combine a global planner (like A\*) with a local reactive layer (like DWA or APF) are also a common and effective pattern for handling dynamic obstacles [Zhang et al., 2025; Liu, Wang, & Xu, 2022].
However, a critical gap remains: the need for a real-time pathfinding algorithm that provides **verifiable safety guarantees** through a hard-constraint mechanism rather than soft penalties, while simultaneously retaining the search efficiency of a bidirectional approach. While many methods aim to avoid obstacles, they do not offer a provable guarantee that a path will not intersect a defined safety radius. This research addresses that specific gap by integrating a novel hard exclusion mechanism directly into a Bidirectional A\* framework, creating a solution designed to balance real-time performance with provable safety in complex urban networks.
Methodology
This section delineates the systematic approach undertaken to develop and evaluate the enhanced bidirectional A\* algorithm. It begins by justifying the core algorithmic choices, then provides an overview of the system architecture and details data acquisition. Subsequently, it elaborates on the core algorithmic components, including the spatial indexing mechanism and the novel hard exclusion strategy, while also addressing their limitations and key implementation details.
3.1. Rationale for Core Design Choices
The selection of the core algorithm was guided by the specific requirements of real-time urban navigation with dynamic obstacles.

- Why Bidirectional A*?: A Bidirectional A* search was chosen over alternatives like Dijkstra's or standard Unidirectional A*. While Dijkstra guarantees the shortest path, its uniform exploration is computationally expensive. A* improves upon this by using a heuristic to guide its search, but a _bidirectional_ approach further optimizes this by searching from both the source and destination simultaneously. The two search frontiers typically meet in the middle, exploring a much smaller total area, which is paramount for achieving real-time performance.
- Why Not Contraction Hierarchies?: We acknowledge that advanced routing algorithms like Contraction Hierarchies (CH) offer superior query times on static networks. However, these methods rely on extensive pre-computation of node hierarchies and shortcuts. This static nature makes them fundamentally ill-suited for handling dynamic obstacles, where edge or node traversability can change frequently, as is central to this research problem. Our chosen approach provides the critical flexibility to incorporate real-time obstacle data without re-processing the entire graph.
- Why Hard Exclusion?: The algorithm employs a hard exclusion strategy, completely pruning any path that violates a given safety margin, rather than a "soft penalty" approach (i.e., increasing edge weights). The rationale is the need for a verifiable safety guarantee. A soft penalty might still produce a path that clips a safety zone if it is sufficiently short, leaving the end-user with an unquantifiable risk. Hard exclusion ensures that any returned path is provably compliant with the specified safety radius, a non-negotiable requirement for safety-critical applications.

  3.3. Data Acquisition and Graph Representation
  The foundational element of this research is a high-fidelity, machine-readable representation of the urban road network.
- Data Source: The study utilizes road network data from OpenStreetMap (OSM), a collaborative, open-source project that provides comprehensive global geospatial data. The specific area of interest encompasses the metropolitan and suburban regions of Kathmandu and Lalitpur, Nepal, chosen for their complex and dense road networks, which provide a challenging and realistic testbed for pathfinding algorithms.
- Graph Preprocessing: The raw OSM data was processed using the Python library OSMnx. OSMnx simplifies the task of downloading, modeling, and analyzing road networks from OSM. It automatically constructs a directed, weighted graph, G = (V, E), where:
  - V is the set of vertices, representing road intersections and dead-ends. Each vertex v ∈ V is a node in the graph, storing its unique OSM node ID and geographic coordinates (latitude, longitude).
  - E is the set of directed edges, representing road segments. Each edge e ∈ E connects two vertices (u, v) and is weighted by its physical length in meters. Edges also retain other metadata from OSM, such as road type and geometry (for curved roads).
- Data Serialization: Following its construction, the graph is serialized into a single file (kathmandu_lalitpur_graph.gpickle) using Python's pickle module. This allows the pre-processed graph to be loaded directly into memory at server startup, amortizing the cost of data acquisition and processing and enabling rapid pathfinding initialization.
  3.4. Spatial Indexing for Efficient Proximity Queries
  Real-time pathfinding in a dense graph necessitates sub-linear time complexity for spatial queries. A naive linear scan through all nodes to find the one nearest to a given coordinate pair would be computationally prohibitive (O(|V|)) and would not meet the system's performance requirements.
- k-d Tree for Node Lookups: To address this, we implement a spatial index using a k-d tree, a space-partitioning data structure for organizing points in a k-dimensional space. At system initialization, the latitude and longitude coordinates of all nodes in V are used to construct a cKDTree from the scipy.spatial library. This indexing structure enables nearest-neighbor queries—for instance, mapping a user's clicked (lat, lon) coordinate to the nearest graph node—in O(log |V|) average-case time complexity.
- Hash-Based Edge Indexing: For constant-time (O(1)) retrieval of edge attributes (e.g., length, geometry), a hash map (defaultdict in Python) is employed. This index maps a source node u to a dictionary of its neighbors v, which in turn stores the data for edge (u, v).
  3.5. The Enhanced Bidirectional A* Algorithm with Hard Exclusion
  The core of our methodology is an enhanced Bidirectional A* algorithm designed to find the shortest path while strictly adhering to the specified safety constraints.

- Baseline Algorithm: The foundation is a Bidirectional A* search, which concurrently explores forward from the source node and backward from the destination node. A path is found when the two search frontiers meet. This approach typically reduces the search space and execution time compared to a unidirectional A* search, as it explores a smaller, more focused area.
- Heuristic Function: The algorithm is guided by a heuristic function, h(n, target), which estimates the cost-to-go from a node n to the target. We employ the Haversine formula as our heuristic. The Haversine distance calculates the great-circle distance between two points on a sphere, providing a geographically accurate, admissible, and consistent straight-line distance estimate for A\*. The choice of a pure distance heuristic is deliberate; obstacle avoidance is not managed by increasing heuristic costs (a soft penalty), but by a hard exclusion mechanism within the search process itself.
- Core Enhancement: Hard Exclusion Mechanism: This mechanism forms the central contribution of our algorithm. It ensures that any path segments violating a specified safety margin are completely pruned from the search space, rather than being disincentivized.
  - Dynamic Obstacles and Tunable Safety Margin: Dynamic obstacles are defined as a set of impassable nodes, O ⊂ V. Each pathfinding request includes a user-configurable `obstacle_radius` (in kilometers), which defines a circular exclusion zone around each obstacle node. This radius provides a tunable parameter to balance safety and path efficiency, allowing users to specify their risk tolerance.

    - Exclusion During Search Expansion: Before any neighbor node is considered for inclusion in the open set, it and its connecting edge undergo a rigorous, three-stage validation process:
    1. Node Obstacle Check: The neighbor node n is checked against the set of obstacle nodes O. If n ∈ O, it is immediately discarded. This is an O(1) check.
    2. Node Proximity Check: The algorithm calculates the Haversine distance from the neighbor node n to every obstacle in O. If this distance is less than the obstacle_radius for any obstacle, the node is discarded. This check prevents the path from terminating on a node that is too close to a hazard. The complexity of this check is O(|O|).
    3. Edge Proximity Check: This is the most critical check for ensuring path safety. It prevents the algorithm from selecting a path that, while connecting two "safe" nodes, passes through an unsafe area between them. The edge (current, neighbor) is modeled as a LineString object using the shapely library. The algorithm then calculates the minimum geometric distance between this line segment and each obstacle Point. If this distance is less than the obstacle_radius, the edge is considered impassable and the neighbor node is discarded from consideration for this path. The complexity of this check is also O(|O|).
    - Limitations and Scalability Considerations: The current implementation of the node and edge proximity checks requires iterating through all active obstacles for each potential node expansion, resulting in a time complexity of O(|O|). We acknowledge this is a potential bottleneck in scenarios with a very high number of obstacles (|O|). Future work could address this by indexing the dynamic obstacles themselves in a secondary k-d tree, which would theoretically reduce the proximity check complexity to O(log |O|).
- Path Reconstruction and Failure Handling: Once the forward and backward search frontiers meet at a meeting_node, a complete path is reconstructed by tracing back the came_from pointers from the meeting node to the source and, separately, to the destination. In the event that both open sets become empty and the frontiers have not met, the algorithm terminates and returns a None value. This signifies that no path exists that satisfies the given obstacle and safety constraints, and this outcome serves as a key metric for evaluating the algorithm's reliability.
  3.6. Caching and Performance Optimization
  To meet real-time requirements, a caching layer is implemented. A unique cache key is generated from the query's signature (source, destination, obstacles, and obstacle_radius). Before initiating a search, the cache is checked for a valid entry. The cache employs a Time-To-Live (TTL) strategy (5 minutes), ensuring a balance between performance gains on repeated queries and responsiveness to changes in the dynamic obstacle set.
  Experimental Setup and Evaluation Metrics  
  This section details the empirical framework designed to rigorously evaluate the performance of the enhanced bidirectional A\* algorithm. The experiments aim to quantify the trade-offs between path safety, efficiency, reliability, and computational cost under various simulated urban traffic conditions.
  4.1. Baseline Algorithm for Comparison

To evaluate the impact of the hard exclusion mechanism, the performance of the proposed algorithm ("Enhanced A\*") is compared against a \*Baseline Bidirectional A\*\*. The baseline is identical but with all obstacle avoidance logic disabled. It computes the geometrically shortest path, representing the ideal path length without constraints.
4.3. Experimental Design
A full factorial experimental design was employed to systematically analyze the algorithm's behavior.

- Independent Variables: Two primary parameters were varied:
  1. Obstacle Density (ρ): To simulate different levels of urban disruption, obstacles were introduced by randomly selecting nodes. Four density levels were tested: ρ = {50, 150, 300, 500} obstacle nodes.
  2. Avoidance Radius (r): To evaluate the tunable safety margin, four preset radius settings were used: 'tight', 'standard', 'wide', and 'very_wide'.
- Experiment Matrix: The combination of 4 obstacle densities and 4 radius settings results in 16 unique experimental configurations. For each configuration, the same set of 100 randomly generated (source, destination) pairs was used, yielding a total of 1,600 experimental runs. This design allows for a robust analysis of the trade-off curves between the evaluation metrics.
  4.4. Evaluation Metrics
  The following metrics were computed for each run to capture the multi-objective trade-offs:
- 4.4.1. Execution Time (ET): The wall-clock time in seconds for the pathfinding function to complete. This measures Computational Cost. Our 'real-time' threshold for this study is an execution time under 250 milliseconds.
- 4.4.2. Failure Rate (FR): The percentage of queries per configuration for which the algorithm failed to find a valid path. This quantifies Reliability.
  FR = (Number of Failed Queries / 100) × 100%
- 4.4.3. Path Length Inflation (PLI): A ratio measuring path Efficiency, calculated as the length of the safe path divided by the baseline path length. This is calculated only for successful runs.
  PLI = Length_yours / Length_baseline
- 4.4.4. Path Vulnerability Index (PVI): A measure of latent Safety, calculated as the percentage of the path's length falling within a "warning zone" of 1.5x the specified obstacle_radius.
- 4.4.5. Search Space Reduction (SSR): A metric quantifying the computational efficiency gained from pruning, measured as the percentage reduction in explored edges compared to the baseline.
  SSR = (1 - (nodes_yours / nodes_baseline)) × 100%

Discussion
This section interprets the empirical results, connecting them to the core research objectives and the broader context of real-time navigation. We analyze the fundamental trade-offs revealed by the experiments, discuss the practical implications of the findings, acknowledge the study's limitations, and propose avenues for future work.

## 6.1. Interpretation of Key Findings

The experimental results provide a clear, quantitative picture of the interplay between the safety guarantees of the hard exclusion mechanism and its impact on path availability and efficiency.

### 6.1.1. The Core Trade-off Triangle: Safety, Efficiency, and Reliability

The results unambiguously illustrate the core trade-off among guaranteed safety, path efficiency, and search reliability. As the avoidance radius (`r`)—our proxy for safety—is increased, both Path Length Inflation (PLI) and Failure Rate (FR) rise, demonstrating a direct cost for larger safety buffers.
For example, at a moderate obstacle density of 150, increasing the radius from 'standard' to 'wide' more than doubles the failure rate (from 14% to 32%) while also increasing the average path detour from 5% to over 12% (PLI of 1.05 vs. 1.12). This relationship intensifies dramatically at higher radii. The 'very_wide' setting, while offering the largest safety margin, proves highly impractical, causing a 54% failure rate at a low density of 50 obstacles, which skyrockets to 96% at 150 obstacles. This shows a clear point of diminishing returns, where the quest for absolute safety renders the system unusable in even moderately congested environments.

### 6.1.2. The "Fail Fast" Performance Characteristic

A crucial and desirable performance characteristic observed is the algorithm's tendency to "fail fast." In highly constrained scenarios—such as high obstacle densities combined with 'wide' or 'very_wide' radii—the average execution time decreases significantly. For instance, at a density of 500, the average time for the 'wide' setting (14.61ms) is dramatically lower than for the 'tight' setting (93.49ms).
This phenomenon occurs because the stringent exclusion criteria cause the algorithm's open sets to be exhausted very quickly, leading to rapid termination when no valid path can be found. This is not a sign of a more efficient search, but rather a feature that prevents the system from becoming unresponsive while searching for a non-existent path. In a real-time application, providing a quick "no path available" response is vastly preferable to a long, fruitless search.

### 6.1.3. Nuanced Insights from the Path Vulnerability Index (PVI)

The PVI offers a more refined measure of path quality beyond simple length or success metrics. The results for the 'tight' and 'standard' settings show the PVI steadily rising with obstacle density (e.g., for 'standard', from 1.21% at 50 obstacles to 8.64% at 500 obstacles). This suggests that as the environment becomes more cluttered, even successful paths are necessarily "squeezed" closer to obstacle warning zones.
The counter-intuitive negative PVI values seen at high densities (e.g., -0.27% for 'wide' at 500 obstacles) are a direct statistical consequence of the extremely high failure rates (96% in that case). This indicates that when the search space is so constrained, the very few paths that succeed are those that exist in sparse, uncontested regions of the graph. These paths are therefore naturally far from any obstacles, resulting in a lower average vulnerability among the small, exceptional set of successful routes.

## 6.2. Implications and Significance of the Research

### 6.2.1. Practical Application: User-Configurable Risk

The findings directly support the design of more sophisticated, user-centric navigation systems. The tunable `obstacle_radius` can be abstracted into an intuitive "safety vs. efficiency" slider. A user could make an informed choice: a 'tight' setting for speed and high reliability, accepting a lower safety margin; or a 'wide' setting to prioritize a verifiable safety buffer, understanding this may result in a longer journey or a higher chance of the system failing to find a route. Our results provide the quantitative data to transparently communicate these trade-offs to the user.

### 6.2.2. Analytical Utility for Urban Planning

This research methodology can be repurposed as an analytical tool for urban resilience assessment. By simulating disruptions (e.g., road closures for events, traffic accidents) as obstacles, city planners can identify critical network bottlenecks. Areas where low obstacle densities lead to high failure rates represent systemic vulnerabilities overly dependent on a few key arteries. This analysis can guide infrastructure investment and emergency response planning.

## 6.3. Limitations of the Current Study

### 6.3.1. Nature of Obstacles

The experiments used fixed sets of randomly generated obstacles for each run. Real-world obstacles are often dynamic (appearing and disappearing) and heterogeneous. Our model's use of static, uniform obstacles for each test run is a simplification that does not capture the temporal dynamics of real-world disruptions.

### 6.3.2. Algorithmic Scalability

As noted in the methodology, the proximity-checking algorithm's O(|O|) complexity per node expansion is a primary bottleneck. The performance in our tests was acceptable for up to 500 obstacles, but this approach would not scale efficiently to scenarios with thousands of simultaneous obstacles without significant optimization.

### 6.3.3. Scope of Evaluation

The evaluation was conducted exclusively on the road network graph of Kathmandu and Lalitpur. While this network's complexity provides a strong test case, the results may not be directly generalizable to all urban layouts (e.g., grid-based cities like Manhattan), which may exhibit different resilience characteristics.

## 6.4. Avenues for Future Research

The limitations of this study point directly to several promising avenues for future work.

### 6.4.1. Immediate Algorithmic Enhancement

The most critical next step is to address the O(|O|) scalability bottleneck. This can be achieved by indexing the dynamic obstacles themselves in a secondary spatial data structure (e.g., a k-d tree). This would theoretically reduce the cost of finding the nearest obstacle to a candidate node or edge to O(log |O|), dramatically improving performance in high-density scenarios.

### 6.4.2. Advanced Modeling of Obstacles

Future work should extend the model to support heterogeneous and time-varying obstacles. This would involve modifying the pathfinding request to accept a list of obstacle objects, each with its own specific radius, and introducing a temporal dimension where obstacles can appear or disappear, requiring the algorithm to re-plan or adapt.

### 6.4.3. System-Level Integration with Predictive Models

A powerful extension would be to integrate the pathfinding system with a predictive model for traffic or hazards. Such a system could move from a purely reactive to a proactive navigation paradigm, routing users around not just current obstacles, but also areas with a high probability of becoming congested or hazardous in the near future.
